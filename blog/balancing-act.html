Balancing Act: Working While Studying, and Not

October 2024 · 6 min read · Vidya Venkappa
  • Career
  • Education
  • Personal

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from closing your laptop after a full day at Cisco, only to open it again minutes later for a data science assignment due before tomorrow's class at the Indian Institute of Science. It is the rhythm of balancing professional work against academic work, and formative in ways that go beyond the transcript.

My path has run through two distinct versions of graduate education: a master's in data science at IISc, India's top-ranked university, completed while working full-time as a software engineer at Cisco; and later, full-time student life in the MS in Information Systems programme at the University of Washington's Foster School of Business. Each offered a different set of challenges and a different set of returns.

Phase one: IISc while working at Cisco

The reality of two professional roles

My days followed a relentless shape. Mornings and afternoons belonged to engineering work at Cisco, evenings to classes at IISc, and weekends to tests and assignments rather than rest.

A typical day:

  • Morning: software engineering work at Cisco
  • Afternoon: meetings, project deadlines, technical deliverables
  • Evening: data science classes and lectures at IISc
  • Night: homework, studying, preparing for near-weekly tests
  • Weekends: intensive study and major assignments

The difficulties were specific:

  • Time management precision. Every hour needed planning around both work deadlines and academic due dates.
  • Mental context-switching. Moving from Cisco engineering problems to academic data science demanded real mental agility.
  • Constant assessment. With tests nearly every weekend, the evaluation cycle never let up.
  • Physical exhaustion. Sustaining focus across two demanding fields required genuine attention to health.
  • No decompression. The work, school and study cycle left very little room for recovery.

The unexpected returns

Immediate application of knowledge. The most valuable part was applying theory directly to engineering work. Learning a clustering algorithm in an evening class and implementing something similar at work the next day created a learning loop that strengthened both sides.

Theory meeting practice. Academic ideas took on new meaning when I could test them against real data and real constraints. I learned not just how algorithms work in theory, but how they behave on enterprise data.

Better problem-solving. Two analytical frameworks running in parallel gave me more than one way into a hard problem.

Accelerated growth. Combining formal education with practical engineering compressed my professional development. I could carry ideas from Friday's lecture into Monday's team discussion.

Value at work. My ongoing education made me more useful to my team. I became a bridge between traditional engineering approaches and newer data-driven methods.

Phase two: full-time study at UW Foster

Moving to full-time study at the University of Washington brought an entirely different set of dynamics.

A new set of challenges

  • Financial pressure. Without a full-time salary, managing expenses while paying for education requires constant attention.
  • Part-time work demands. Balancing part-time work against coursework creates its own scheduling problems.
  • Job search anxiety. The pressure of finding post-graduation employment adds a layer of stress to everything academic.
  • Future uncertainty. Making career decisions without the security of full-time employment carries a real psychological cost.

The unexpected returns

  • Deeper exploration. I can go further into concepts and technologies that interest me without the constraint of immediate work relevance.
  • Portfolio development. Time to build comprehensive projects that actually demonstrate what I can do.
  • Broader skills. Not being limited to work-relevant technologies means I can explore widely.
  • Academic immersion. Being fully present in the environment allows stronger connections with professors and peers.
  • Room to experiment. I can take more risk with projects and learning paths.

Strategies that worked in both

Adaptive time management. During the Cisco and IISc period I used calendar blocking with something close to military precision. As a full-time student with part-time work, I adapted that into flexible blocks that accommodate a variable schedule while protecting the key deadlines.

Leveraging complementary skills. At Cisco I deliberately connected coursework to engineering projects. Now I look for part-time work that complements my academic focus.

Strategic networking. During the master's I connected with professionals interested in applied data science. As a full-time student I have expanded that toward potential employers and industry mentors.

Sustainable routines. The relentless weekend tests forced strict study routines. Those same habits now drive consistent project development despite variable hours.

What each path actually taught me

Working while studying

Time management mastery, out of necessity. Work-life balance as an active skill rather than a phrase. Real resilience, built from handling weekend tests without dropping professional performance. An applied learning mindset. And the habit of finding ways to connect workplace problems to academic solutions.

Full-time study

A different kind of balance: the pressure is real but the quality of life is better. Self-directed motivation, in the absence of an externally imposed structure. Financial discipline. Career navigation as a skill in its own right. And the ability to build and present a portfolio that shows capability rather than asserting it.

Advice, depending on which way you are going

If you are considering working while studying

Align your job and your studies if you can. My data science coursework complemented my engineering role directly, and that synergy did most of the work. Look for it deliberately.

Prepare for intense weekends. If your programme runs regular weekend assessments, your weekends are for studying. Adjust your social expectations up front rather than being surprised.

Use your workplace for academic projects. Where appropriate, workplace challenges make excellent foundations for coursework: efficient, and grounded in something real.

Communicate boundaries clearly. Be upfront with managers and professors about the dual commitment before either side forms unrealistic expectations.

If you are considering full-time study

Budget meticulously. Without steady income, detailed financial planning is the single largest lever on your anxiety level.

Seek strategic part-time work. Look for roles that complement your studies and career goals, even where they pay less than the alternatives.

Start the job search early. Preparing from day one is far easier than compressing it near graduation.

Create your own structure. Without workplace demands, productivity depends entirely on structure you impose yourself.

Conclusion: it comes down to priorities

Having done both, I do not think there is a universally better approach. It is about matching your educational path to your priorities and your life stage.

During my time at Cisco I gained time management skills and learned to balance work against everything else. As a full-time student I found a different rhythm with a better overall quality of life, financial pressure and job search notwithstanding.

The insight I keep returning to is simple: learning is important either way. Whether you are applying concepts in a corporate environment or building projects as a full-time student, the value comes from engaging fully with the material and doing work that means something.

Some people thrive under the pressure and practical application of working while studying. Others flourish with the depth that full-time study allows. What I can say is that both experiences made me more capable and more adaptable. The technical skills were valuable, but the meta-skills built by navigating two very different models may end up mattering more.
